# Who Is the Importer of Record When You Fulfil from China? Probably You.

![A warehouse worker in Shenzhen handing a sealed carton and its commercial invoice to a courier at the dispatch door, the point where the shipment leaves the warehouse's hands](/images/blog/importer-of-record-china-fulfillment-2026.webp)

> **Quick answer:** The importer of record is the party legally responsible for an import: the declaration, the classification, the value, the duty and compliance. Under **DDP** that is normally **you, the seller**, directly or through a licensed broker or customs representative. It is not your 3PL or forwarder just because they handled the goods: in the US, only the owner or purchaser of the goods, or a licensed broker they appoint, can be importer of record. In the **EU**, the seller becomes the importer on distance sales under the new Customs Code from 2028. In the **UK**, you already collect VAT at checkout on parcels under £135, and duty liability follows by 2029.

Ask most sellers who imports their goods and they will name their forwarder, their 3PL, or "whoever does the DDP".

That answer is usually wrong, and it tends to come to light at the worst possible moment: a shipment held at the border, a customs authority asking questions, and a seller discovering that the party answering for the declaration was them all along.

## What the importer of record actually is

The importer of record is the party a customs authority holds responsible for an import. That responsibility covers:

- **Declaring the goods** accurately
- **Classifying** them under the right tariff code
- **Valuing** them correctly
- **Paying** the duty and import taxes owed
- **Making sure the goods comply** with the importing country's rules, from product safety to labelling

When a declaration is wrong, it is the importer of record who answers for it. Not the warehouse that packed the carton, and not the carrier that flew it.

## The rule in one line

**The importer of record is whoever the import is declared in the name of, and the trade terms decide who that should be.**

| Trade term | Who handles import clearance and duty | Usually the importer |
|---|---|---|
| **DDP**, Delivered Duty Paid | The seller | **The seller** |
| **DAP**, Delivered at Place | The buyer | **The buyer** |

If you sell direct to consumers and want them to receive a parcel with nothing to pay, you are selling DDP. You have taken on the import, and with it, the role. Our guide to [DDP shipping from China](ddp-shipping-from-china-explained-2026.html) covers how duty is collected and paid under that model.

## The United States

US law is specific about who is allowed to be importer of record. Under **19 USC 1484**, it is:

- **the owner or purchaser** of the goods, which CBP reads as any party with a genuine financial interest in the transaction, or
- **a licensed customs broker**, when designated by the owner, purchaser or consignee.

No one else qualifies. CBP has ruled on parties that tried to act as importer of record without enough financial interest in the goods, and found they could not.

That has a direct consequence for how you think about your service providers. **A warehouse that stores and packs your goods has no financial interest in them.** It does not become your importer of record by shipping them, and it cannot take the role off you.

To act as importer of record you need an **importer number**. For a US company that is usually its IRS EIN. For a foreign company with no US tax number, CBP assigns one through **Form 5106**. A company outside the US can be the importer of record, and commonly files through a licensed US customs broker. **Formal entries**, generally those over $2,500, also need a **customs bond**.

The detail on that form now matters more than it used to. Since 18 September 2026, CBP voids importer numbers where the Form 5106 data is inaccurate, including where the physical address is a broker's or forwarder's. Our piece on [Form 5106 and voided importer numbers](cbp-form-5106-importer-number-voided-2026.html) explains what to check.

## The European Union

The EU is in the middle of changing who the importer is.

**Today**, on low-value parcels sold to consumers, the consumer is generally treated as the importer, and a seller using **IOSS** collects the import VAT at checkout so the parcel clears without the customer paying again on delivery. Since 1 July 2026 a flat €3 customs duty also applies to parcels under €150, which our [EU de minimis guide](eu-de-minimis-2026-customs-duty-guide.html) covers.

**Under the new Union Customs Code**, Regulation (EU) 2026/2108, that flips. For distance sales from outside the EU, the importer becomes the **importer for distance sales**: the seller supplying the goods, or the platform facilitating the sale. The e-commerce changes are tied to **1 July 2028**. We cover the full timeline in our piece on [the EU customs reform](eu-customs-reform-importer-distance-sales-2026.html).

One structural point applies either way. A business established outside the EU can be the importer, but it generally cannot lodge the declaration itself. It needs an **EU EORI number** if it acts in its own name, and an **indirect customs representative** established in the EU to make declarations on its behalf.

## The United Kingdom

The UK already puts much of the burden on the seller.

**For consignments of £135 or less** sold to UK consumers, the overseas seller charges **UK VAT at checkout**, registers with HMRC and accounts for it. Where an online marketplace facilitates the sale, the marketplace does this instead. Those consignments currently pay **no customs duty**.

**Above £135**, import VAT and any duty are collected at the border in the usual way, and under DDP that falls to you.

**That duty relief is going.** The UK government intends to remove the customs duty relief on consignments under £135 **by March 2029 at the latest**, with online sellers and marketplaces becoming liable for the duty. If you sell into the UK on the assumption that small parcels pay no duty, that assumption has a date on it. Our [China to UK fulfilment guide](china-to-uk-fulfilment-shopify.html) covers the current position in full.

## Side by side

| | Today, under DDP | What you need | What is changing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **United States** | You, or a licensed broker you designate | Importer number, bond for formal entries | Form 5106 enforcement since 18 Sep 2026 |
| **European Union** | The consumer on low-value IOSS parcels; you on higher value DDP | EU EORI and an indirect representative if you are the importer | You or the platform become importer, e-commerce changes from 1 Jul 2028 |
| **United Kingdom** | You collect VAT under £135; you are importer above it | HMRC VAT registration | Duty relief under £135 removed by Mar 2029 |

## The cheap DDP quote that should worry you

Here is the practical warning this article exists to give.

Some low-cost "DDP" shipping from China works by entering your goods under **someone else's** importer number, often at a value lower than you paid. The service looks simple and cheap because the hard part has been done for you, out of sight.

The problem is that **you lose sight of what was declared about your goods**. If the value was understated or the classification was wrong, it is your goods that get held, your shipment that stops, and your customers who wait. And in the US, a party with no financial interest in the goods is not entitled to be importer of record in the first place.

So when a DDP quote looks unusually cheap, ask two questions before you accept it:

1. **Whose name and importer number do the goods enter under?**
2. **What value is being declared?**

If the answers are vague, the price is not the bargain it looks.

We are a fulfilment provider in Shenzhen. We store, pack and ship, and we prepare the documents that travel with your goods.

**We are not your importer of record, we cannot be, and we are not a licensed customs broker or customs representative.** We have no financial interest in your goods. Under US law that alone rules us out of the role, and the same principle holds elsewhere: handling a parcel does not make anyone its importer.

What we can do is make sure the information leaving our warehouse is accurate: the description, the value, the tariff code and the origin, consistent from invoice to carton. Whoever acts as your importer of record is relying on that information, and so is any customs authority reviewing it. It is the core of our [tariff management service](tariff-management-china.html).

For the role itself, use a licensed customs broker in the US, and an indirect customs representative or adviser in the EU and UK. That is their work, not ours.

## The bottom line

If you sell DDP to consumers from China, you are almost certainly the importer of record, or about to become it. In the US the law already says so, in the UK you already collect the tax, and in the EU the new Customs Code is moving the role to you.

That is not a reason to worry. It is a reason to know whose name is on your declarations, check that the information behind them is right, and choose providers who will tell you plainly where their responsibility ends.
