# Is De Minimis Back? Shipping D2C Orders from China to the US in 2027

![A smiling customer at her front door taking a parcel from a courier, the doorstep moment DDP shipping keeps free of any duty bill](/images/blog/shipping-d2c-orders-china-to-us-2027.webp)

> **Quick answer:** **No.** The $800 de minimis exemption is still suspended for goods from every country, and Congress has repealed it in law from **1 July 2027**. When the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026, an executive order kept the suspension the same day, and since **24 June 2026** CBP has kept it under its own rules. So every D2C parcel from China still needs a customs entry and pays duty: the normal MFN rate, any Section 301 list duty and, since 24 July 2026, the **12.5% Section 301 forced labor duty**. The IEEPA tariffs are gone and are being refunded. From **20 January 2027**, China-origin parcels sent by post must use CBP's new **Entry Type 13** or a formal entry. D2C from China still works for US customers if you ship DDP, classify every SKU correctly and price the duty in.

Since the Supreme Court ruling in February, US-facing brands have been asking the same thing: the court threw out the tariffs, so is de minimis back?

It is a fair question, because the original suspension rested on the same emergency-powers law the court struck down. The answer is still no. Why it is still no tells you how long to plan for, and what each parcel to a US customer actually pays.

## Why de minimis did not come back

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| 2 May 2025 | Duty-free de minimis ends for goods from China and Hong Kong |
| 29 Aug 2025 | The suspension is extended to low-value shipments from all countries |
| 20 Feb 2026 | Supreme Court rules IEEPA does not authorise tariffs; an executive order the same day keeps de minimis suspended |
| 24 Feb 2026 | CBP stops collecting IEEPA duties |
| 28 Feb 2026 | Postal parcels move to percentage-of-value duty only |
| 24 Jun 2026 | CBP suspends de minimis indefinitely under its own authority; for mail, with a new postal entry process, from 24 July |
| 24 Jul 2026 | A 12.5% Section 301 forced labor duty on China-origin goods takes effect |
| 20 Jan 2027 | Entry Type 13 becomes mandatory for postal shipments subject to Section 301 duties |
| 1 Jul 2027 | The statutory repeal of de minimis takes effect |

Three separate things keep the door shut. CBP now suspends the exemption under its own regulations, not under the emergency law the court struck down. Congress repealed it in law in 2025, with effect from 1 July 2027. And the court's ruling was about tariffs, not about de minimis, so it touched neither.

**Plan as if there is no duty-free threshold for US parcels**, because there is not one now, and from July 2027 there will not be one in law either. Our earlier guide to [the end of US de minimis](us-de-minimis-ended-2026-china-sellers-guide.html) covers how the suspension began.

## What a D2C parcel from China pays now

Every parcel pays up to three layers of duty, plus fees:

1. **The MFN duty rate** for its HTS code, which is the normal US tariff
2. **A Section 301 list duty**, if the product is on one of the China lists, from 7.5% up to 100% depending on the list
3. **The 12.5% Section 301 forced labor duty**, on top, for China-origin goods unless they are exempt
4. **Fees**: CBP's processing fee on the entry, and the carrier's clearance or disbursement charge

What is no longer there is the IEEPA layer, the "fentanyl" and "reciprocal" tariffs that applied for most of 2025. Those are gone, and duty paid under them is being refunded to importers of record; our [IEEPA refund guide](ieepa-tariff-refunds-china-sellers-2026.html) explains who gets that money.

Here is an example parcel with a declared value of $25, for a product with a 5% MFN rate that sits on Section 301 List 4A at 7.5%. For a sale direct to a consumer, the declared value is usually the price your customer paid for the goods.

| Layer | Rate | Duty |
|---|---|---|
| MFN duty | 5% | $1.25 |
| Section 301, List 4A | 7.5% | $1.88 |
| Section 301 forced labor duty | 12.5% | $3.13 |
| **Total duty** | **25%** | **$6.26** |

That is a quarter of the declared value, before fees and shipping. Your own rates depend on the HTS code, and our [Section 301 forced labor guide](section-301-forced-labor-tariff-china-2026.html) explains what stacks and what is exempt.

## How the duty is collected, by shipping method

**Express courier.** Parcels valued at $2,500 or less clear on an informal entry. Under DDP, the courier bills the duty to the shipper. Under DAP, it asks your customer for the duty plus a handling fee before delivery. A CBP proposal published on 8 October 2026 would require these informal entries to be filed on or before the parcel's arrival rather than within 15 days. That is a proposal, with comments due by 7 December 2026, not a rule in force.

**Postal.** Duty on postal parcels is collected by the carrier or a CBP-certified "qualified party", and since 28 February 2026 only as a percentage of value. The bigger change is coming: **from 20 January 2027, postal shipments subject to Section 301 duties, which covers goods from China, must use Entry Type 13 or a formal entry**. CBP moved that date from 22 October 2026 and says there are no changes to mail entry procedures before it. Expect postal lines from China to ask for more data, such as sender details and tracking numbers, and possibly to reprice. Ask your provider now whether its postal service will be ready on 20 January.

**Bulk import to a US warehouse.** If you bring stock in bulk and fulfil from inside the US, duty is paid once on a formal entry, on the value of the bulk shipment rather than on each retail sale. That is the basis of the [hybrid model](china-us-hybrid-fulfillment-model-2026.html), and it is worth running the numbers for your fastest sellers.

## DDP is the only sensible setting for US D2C

A parcel that arrives with a bill attached is a parcel that often comes back. Customers refuse it, file a chargeback, or simply never buy again. Ship **DDP**, so the duty is settled before delivery, and say "duties included" on your product pages and at checkout so customers know there is nothing to pay at the door. Our [DDP explainer](ddp-shipping-from-china-explained-2026.html) covers how it works from Shenzhen.

## Does shipping D2C from China still make sense for US customers?

Often, yes. But it is a test you run per product, not per store.

**The duty base is the big difference.** Shipped D2C, duty is charged on each parcel's declared value, usually the retail price. Imported in bulk, it is charged on the wholesale value. Take a product your customer pays $40 for that cost you $10 from the factory, at the 25% combined rate from the example above:

| | Duty base | Duty per unit |
|---|---|---|
| Shipped D2C from China | $40 retail value | $10.00 |
| Imported in bulk, fulfilled in the US | $10 wholesale value | $2.50 |

Bulk import is not automatically cheaper overall. It means paying for US warehousing, a domestic 3PL's fees and the freight to get there, and buying the stock months earlier. Our [D2C vs US 3PL cost audit](direct-from-china-d2c-vs-us-3pl-2026.html) works through those lines.

**D2C from China usually wins** for light, higher-margin products, wide catalogues where most SKUs sell slowly, new products you are testing, and crowdfunding rewards. **Bulk import usually wins** for heavy, low-margin bestsellers that sell in volume every week. Many brands do both: bestsellers in the US, everything else direct from China.

## Your checklist for the first quarter of 2027

1. **Re-check every HTS code and the duty per SKU**, including the 12.5% forced labor duty.
2. **Ship DDP** and show "duties included" to customers.
3. **Ask your postal provider about Entry Type 13** readiness for 20 January 2027.
4. **Price the duty in.** Build it into the product price rather than surprising customers with it later.
5. **Split the catalogue**: D2C from China for the long tail, bulk to the US for heavy bestsellers.
6. **Check what you are owed** from 2025's IEEPA tariffs.
7. **Watch the CBP low-value proposal**, with comments due 7 December 2026.

De minimis is not coming back: it is suspended under CBP's own rules today and repealed in law from July 2027.
