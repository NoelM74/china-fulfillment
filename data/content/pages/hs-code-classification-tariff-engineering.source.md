# HS Code Classification and Tariff Engineering: A 2026 Guide for Importers

![An importer reviewing HTS classification codes and duty rates against product samples, with a Shenzhen warehouse and outbound parcels in the background](/images/blog/hs-code-classification-tariff-engineering.webp)

> **Quick answer:** Your duty rate is set by one thing, the HTS code your product is classified under when it crosses the border. Getting that code right is a legal obligation. Designing a product so that it lawfully falls under a better code is called tariff engineering, and US courts have upheld it since 1882. The line is simple to state and easy to cross: the product must genuinely be what you say it is at the moment of import. Classification and valuation are the importer of record's responsibility, so treat anyone who offers to make your duty problem disappear with suspicion.

Most sellers importing from China think about duty as a fixed cost of doing business. It is not. It is the output of a classification decision, and that decision is frequently made by whoever fills in the paperwork fastest, usually a supplier or a forwarder with no stake in whether the code is right.

That matters more in 2026 than it did five years ago, because the amount riding on each digit has gone up sharply.

## What the duty stack actually looks like

Verified as at 31 August 2026. Tariff policy has changed repeatedly this year, so check current rates before you price anything.

| Layer | Rate on Chinese goods | Status |
|---|---|---|
| MFN base duty | Set by HTS code, roughly 0% to 37.5% | In force, varies enormously by product |
| Section 301, 2018 lists | 25% on Lists 1 to 3, 7.5% on List 4A | In force |
| Section 301, forced labour | 12.5% | In force from 24 July 2026 |
| Section 232 | 25% to 50% on steel, aluminium, copper, vehicles | In force where applicable |
| IEEPA tariffs | Was 10% fentanyl plus 10% reciprocal | Struck down 20 February 2026 |
| Section 122 global surcharge | Was 15% | Expired 24 July 2026 |

Two things happened this year that changed the picture completely.

On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise the President to impose tariffs. Every IEEPA tariff fell with that ruling. CBP has been processing refunds since, through a system called CAPE, and by 31 July 2026 around $128.68 billion in refunds had been accepted for processing. If you imported from China during that window and have not looked at whether you are owed money, that is worth an afternoon of someone's time.

Then on 24 July 2026 two things happened in the same minute. The temporary Section 122 surcharge expired, and a new Section 301 duty of 12.5% took effect on Chinese goods, arising from an investigation into the enforcement of forced labour import prohibitions. China sits in the 12.5% band alongside Brazil, Vietnam and Russia.

The practical result is that the headline burden did not fall much, but its legal basis moved from a contested authority to a much more settled one. Section 301 duties have survived court challenge repeatedly. Planning on the assumption that they will disappear is not a plan.

The part that has not changed at all is the base layer. Your MFN rate still comes from your HTS code, and that is the layer you have any legitimate influence over.

## How classification actually works

The Harmonized System is an international framework. The first six digits are the same everywhere, and countries add their own digits after that. The US version is the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States, and it runs to ten digits.

Classification is not a matter of opinion. It follows six General Rules of Interpretation, applied in order.

**GRI 1** is the one that decides most cases. You classify according to the terms of the headings and any relevant section or chapter notes. If a heading plainly describes your product, you stop there. Most people who get classification wrong get it wrong here, by reaching for a code that sounds right rather than reading the notes that govern it.

**GRI 2** covers incomplete or unassembled goods, and mixtures. An article shipped disassembled is generally classified as the finished article if it has its essential character as presented.

**GRI 3** handles goods that could fall under two or more headings. The more specific description wins. If that does not resolve it, you classify by essential character. If that still does not resolve it, the heading that appears last in numerical order wins.

**GRI 4** is a fallback for goods most akin to something else, and is rarely used.

**GRI 5** deals with cases and packing materials.

**GRI 6** applies the same logic at subheading level.

The reason this matters commercially is that GRI 3 essential character determinations are where large duty differences live, and where two competent people can reach different conclusions on the same product. That is not a loophole. It is a genuine ambiguity in a system trying to sort every physical object into a finite list.

## Where classification quietly goes wrong

Some categories are far more error prone than others, because the code turns on physical attributes that nobody thinks to record.

**Apparel** is classified by fibre content, construction and gender, and rates swing hard. A garment that is 50% cotton and 50% polyester classifies differently from one that is 60% polyester. Knit and woven go to different chapters entirely. If you are running an [apparel or fashion brand out of China](apparel-fashion-fulfillment-from-china-2026.html), the fibre breakdown on your tech pack is a duty document whether you treat it as one or not.

**Footwear** turns on the constituent material of the upper and the outer sole, whether the upper covers the ankle, and in some headings the value per pair. Chapter 64 rates range from a few percent to 37.5%.

**Sets and kits** are a common trap. When you bundle several items for retail sale, GRI 3 asks which component gives the set its essential character, and the whole set may take that component's rate. A set assembled at source is a different classification question from the same items shipped loose, which is worth understanding before you commit to [source side kitting and bundle assembly](source-side-kitting-bundle-assembly-china.html).

**Parts versus accessories** is a persistent problem in electronics and machinery. Section and chapter notes often exclude parts from the heading you would expect, and route them somewhere else entirely.

**Electronics with batteries** can shift classification based on whether the battery is installed, included separately, or absent, which interacts with the [dangerous goods rules for shipping batteries](shipping-batteries-electronics-from-china-2026.html) in ways that surprise people.

The common thread is that the deciding attribute is usually a manufacturing detail sitting in a spec sheet, not something visible on a commercial invoice.

## Tariff engineering, and where the line sits

Tariff engineering is the practice of designing or specifying a product so that it lawfully falls under a more favourable classification. CBP itself describes it as the longstanding principle that merchandise is classified in its condition as imported, and that an importer has the right to fashion merchandise to obtain the lowest rate of duty.

That principle is old and well settled.

In **Merritt v. Welsh** (1882) the Supreme Court dealt with sugar refiners who manufactured sugar in darker colours because the tariff was keyed to colour. The Court was unimpressed by the argument that this was evasion. Its answer was direct: has not a manufacturer a right to make his goods as he pleases?

In **United States v. Citroen** (1912) an importer brought in drilled but unstrung pearls. Strung pearls attracted 60% duty, unstrung pearls 10%. The evidence showed plainly that the pearls were designed, marketed and imported to be strung into jewellery. The Court held they were still classifiable as unstrung, because that was their condition at import.

The modern illustration of the same principle working is **Converse**, which adds felt to the outsoles of certain shoes. The felt changes the classification, the shoes function with it, and consumers buy them exactly as imported. Nothing is undone after clearance.

Then there is the counter example, and it is expensive.

**Ford** imported Transit Connect vans from Turkey fitted with a second row of seats, declaring them as passenger vehicles at 2.5% rather than cargo vehicles at the 25% chicken tax rate. After customs clearance, and before the vehicles left the port, a subcontractor removed the second row seats and the vans were delivered as cargo vans. CBP called the seats an improper artifice or disguise masking the true nature of the vehicle at importation. The Court of International Trade sided with Ford. The Federal Circuit reversed. Ford ultimately settled for $365 million.

The distinction is not subtle once you see it. Converse sells the shoe it imported. Ford did not sell the vehicle it imported. The seats existed to get through the border and served no purpose afterwards.

A workable test, and one worth applying honestly before you spend money on a redesign:

- Does the product genuinely function as imported, in the form it was declared?
- Is that form what the customer actually receives?
- Would the change survive being described in full, in writing, to CBP?
- Is there a commercial reason for the change beyond duty, even a modest one?
- If the change were reversed after clearance, would the product be materially different?

If you cannot answer the first three cleanly, you are not doing tariff engineering. You are building a penalty case against yourself under 19 USC 1592, where the statutory maximum is 20% of the domestic value of the goods for negligence, 40% for gross negligence, and the entire domestic value for fraud.

Genuine tariff engineering is also a product decision, not a logistics decision. It happens at the design and specification stage with your factory, ideally with trade counsel involved before tooling is committed. Anyone offering to do it for you after the goods exist is describing something else.

## Valuation is the other half

Duty is a rate multiplied by a value, and importers spend almost all their attention on the rate.

Where your supply chain runs through a trading company or sourcing agent, the **first sale rule** may allow you to declare the factory's price to the middleman rather than the middleman's price to you. On a genuine multi tiered transaction this reduces the dutiable value directly.

It is legitimate, long established, and heavily scrutinised. You must declare at entry that transaction value is based on an earlier sale, a requirement that has been mandatory since 2016. You need contemporaneous documentation, created at the time of the transaction rather than assembled later for an audit. CBP has been issuing first sale questionnaires and reviewing programmes actively. If your records are reconstructed after the fact, first sale becomes a liability rather than a saving.

This is trade counsel territory, not something to attempt from a blog post.

## Getting certainty instead of an opinion

If a classification genuinely matters to your margin, stop guessing.

Start with **CROSS**, CBP's Customs Rulings Online Search System at rulings.cbp.gov. It holds decades of rulings, searchable by keyword and HTS number. There is a reasonable chance CBP has already ruled on a product close enough to yours to settle the question.

If nothing fits, request a **binding ruling** through the eRulings portal. You describe the product in detail, propose a classification, and CBP issues a ruling that binds it at every port. Acknowledgement and a control number arrive within one business day. The National Commodity Specialist Division aims to issue most classification rulings within 30 calendar days. Requests referred to Headquarters run to around 90 days, and genuinely novel products can take 120 to 180 days or more.

A binding ruling is the single most underused tool available to small importers. It costs nothing but the time to prepare a good request, and it converts an argument into a documented position. It is also the correct way to test a tariff engineering idea before you commit to it, rather than after.

The trade off worth understanding: a ruling binds you as well as CBP. If the answer comes back worse than the code you have been using, you now know, and you are expected to act on it.

## What fulfilling from China does and does not change

Being clear about this matters, because the topic attracts a lot of overclaiming.

Fulfilment from origin **does not** change your duty rate. The rate follows classification and origin. Nobody can ship your way into a different HTS code.

What it does change is timing and structure.

Under a bulk import model you pay duty on an entire container before a single unit has sold. That is working capital committed to a tax on inventory you may not move. Shipping to the customer as orders arrive means duty is assessed shipment by shipment, in step with revenue rather than ahead of it. That is a cash flow difference, not a duty saving, and it is worth stating plainly as the former.

It also does not avoid duty on low value parcels any more. The $800 de minimis exemption has been suspended since 2025, the Court of International Trade upheld the suspension in *Axle of Dearborn* on 13 August 2026, and Congress repealed the statutory exemption outright with effect from 1 July 2027. Anyone still selling a de minimis based strategy for the US market is selling something that no longer exists. We covered the detail in our [guide to the end of US de minimis](us-de-minimis-ended-2026-china-sellers-guide.html), and the parallel EU changes in the [EU de minimis guide](eu-de-minimis-2026-customs-duty-guide.html).

Because duty is now unavoidable on US bound parcels, the practical question has shifted to who pays it and when the customer finds out. That is an Incoterms decision, and for direct to consumer parcels the answer is almost always [DDP](ddp-shipping-from-china-explained-2026.html). A customer hit with an unexpected customs bill at the door is a refund and a chargeback, not a sale.

Where a fulfilment partner genuinely helps is unglamorous: accurate product data, consistent declared values, correct weights and dimensions, complete commercial invoices, materials and fibre content recorded properly at intake, and records you can produce years later when someone asks. Bad data at origin is where a large share of classification errors are actually born.

## The bottom line

Duty is not a fixed cost. It is the result of a classification decision, and that decision deserves more attention than it usually gets.

Get the code right first, because most of the money lost to classification is lost to plain error rather than missed cleverness. Understand that tariff engineering is real, lawful and old, and that its entire legitimacy rests on the product genuinely being what you declared at the moment it crossed the border. Use binding rulings to convert uncertainty into a documented position. Keep the valuation side as clean as the classification side.

And keep in mind that the rates in this guide are current as at 31 August 2026, in a year that has already seen the Supreme Court strike down one tariff regime and a new one take its place within five months.
