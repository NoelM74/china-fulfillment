# Facebook posts — week of 8 September 2026

Facebook adaptations of the four LinkedIn posts. Same facts, different shape.

## What changed and why

Facebook is not a shorter LinkedIn. Three differences drive the rewrite:

- **The fold is much earlier.** Facebook cuts to "See more" around 125 characters on mobile, against roughly 200 on LinkedIn. The first line has to carry more.
- **Comments in the first 30 to 60 minutes matter more than almost anything else** for reach. So each post ends on something a real person might actually answer, not a limp "Thoughts?".
- **The reader is scrolling, not researching.** Long structured argument is a LinkedIn behaviour. These run roughly half the length and lose the sub-headed structure.

Everything factual is unchanged and still traceable to the published pages.

## POST 1 — Dimensional weight

USPS changed how it charges for parcel size in July. If you ship anything bulky, you are already paying more for it.

Two changes. The divisor moved from 166 to 139, which reporting at the time put at roughly 19 percent more billable weight. And fractions now round up instead of to the nearest inch, so a 12.1 inch carton bills as 13.

It only applies to parcels over one cubic foot, which makes that a useful line to design your packaging around.

The thing worth understanding: dimensional weight charges you for the space a box takes up, not what is inside it. A 30 x 30 x 30 box with a 400g product in it is not a 400g parcel as far as your carrier is concerned. You are paying to ship air.

Worst affected are the predictable ones. Apparel, bedding, toys, pet products, multipacks, and anything in a box picked for how it looks rather than how it fits.

The fix is not a cheaper carrier. It is a smaller box.

Most brands run three or four carton sizes because that is what they ordered years ago, then keep using them as the range grows. Check your actual product dimensions against your actual box sizes. There is almost always something shipping a size too big on every single order.

You renegotiate carrier rates once a year. You choose a box size on every order.

When did you last audit your carton sizes?

## POST 2 — Returns

"But what about returns?" is the number one reason brands rule out fulfilling from China.

It is also the easiest worry on that list to fix.

The version people picture is the broken one: a customer in Ohio posts a $25 item all the way back to Shenzhen. That costs more than the item is worth, takes weeks, and arrives as something you have to discount anyway. Fair enough. Nobody should run it that way.

So flip it. Your customer never returns to China. They return to a local address in their own country, fast and cheap, exactly what they expect. The decisions happen there, in bulk.

Every returned item then goes one of three ways, and it is a sum rather than a habit. Resellable where it landed, put it back into local stock. Worth recovering but not resellable there, hold it and send a consolidated pallet back to Shenzhen every so often. Neither of those, liquidate it locally, because paying to fly a dead unit across the world is throwing good money after bad.

Two things most sellers miss. Set a value below which you just refund and let the customer keep it, because getting some items back costs more than they are worth. And look into duty drawback, since you are now paying import duty on US-bound goods and part of it may be recoverable on anything re-exported.

Build the returns loop on day one. It is much harder to retrofit once they start stacking up.

What is your return rate running at?

## POST 3 — Incoterms and EXW

The cheapest quote your supplier gives you is usually the most expensive one.

EXW looks lower on the proforma because the origin costs have been taken out of it. They have not gone anywhere. You pay them later, separately, and by then they are yours to sort out.

Inland haulage to the port. Chinese export clearance and paperwork. Terminal handling. Origin agent fees.

Add all of that back and EXW often ends up higher than the FOB price for the same goods, with more admin and more ways to be held up.

There is a second cost that never shows on an invoice. Under EXW, if export clearance goes wrong, that is your problem, in a country where you have no standing and probably no Mandarin. Under FOB the supplier owns that leg, and they are actually there.

EXW does make sense in one case. If you already have a forwarder in China handling the origin leg for you, you are basically rebuilding FOB with someone you chose instead of someone your supplier chose. That is a good reason.

Wanting a smaller number on the proforma is not.

Comparing supplier quotes this week? Add the origin costs back onto every EXW price before you compare anything. The order often changes.

## POST 4 — The question providers dread

Ask any fulfilment company this one question and you will learn more than a sales call will ever tell you.

"Which parts will you not handle?"

An honest answer from any China 3PL has to include these. You are the importer of record. You arrange your own customs bond if one is needed. Product testing and certification sit with you and your manufacturer, through accredited labs. Tax registration is yours, whether that is UK or EU VAT, IOSS, or Japanese consumption tax.

A warehouse can prep your goods properly, document them accurately and hand them to a carrier. It cannot become your legal importer or certify your product. Anyone suggesting otherwise is either confused or hoping you are, and you will find out which at the worst possible moment.

One more worth stealing, because it is quietly brutal: "can you describe my marketplace's prep spec without looking it up?"

Amazon, Walmart and TikTok Shop all have their own labelling, bagging and carton rules, and they differ in exactly the details that get shipments rejected. Someone who does this every day will talk fluently about FNSKU placement, suffocation warnings and box content accuracy. Someone who does not will say "full prep services".

Ask both of everyone you are considering. Us included.

# Hashtags: the short answer is mostly no

Checked against current guidance rather than habit, because this changed materially in the last year.

## LinkedIn: skip them

LinkedIn has **dismantled the hashtag system**. It removed the ability to follow hashtags, dropped hashtag fields from profiles, and retired Creator Mode hashtag topics. Discovery now runs on LLM-based semantic retrieval, which reads what your post is actually about rather than what you tagged it.

The performance data is genuinely contradictory. Some analyses report posts without hashtags outperforming those with them by 5 to 10 percent; others report a 2 to 9 percent lift from using three to five. When credible sources disagree that sharply in both directions, the honest read is that the effect is small either way and swamped by everything else.

What is not in dispute is the structural change. Following is gone, semantic retrieval is in. **Keywords in your body copy now teach the algorithm what you write about far more reliably than tags appended at the end.**

Your posts already do this without trying. "Dimensional weight", "importer of record", "FNSKU", "IOSS", "duty drawback", "Incoterms" all appear naturally in the copy because the posts are genuinely about those things. That is the modern version of hashtag targeting, and you are already doing it.

**Recommendation: no hashtags on LinkedIn.** The upside is a rounding error, and three tags at the bottom of an otherwise well-written post makes it look like marketing rather than expertise. That matters more for you than the 2 percent, because the whole positioning of these posts is that a practitioner wrote them.

**The one exception:** a live industry event or a moment with a real shared tag, where targeted hashtags can genuinely aggregate a conversation. Not applicable to any of these four.

## Facebook: two or three at most, and it barely matters

Hashtags on Facebook are **not a meaningful ranking factor** and have not been for years. They do something narrow: a tapped or searched hashtag surfaces public posts, which has some value inside Groups and niche communities. Adding five to every post in the hope the algorithm rewards it does nothing.

Two mechanical points if you do use them. The post must be **public** for hashtags to appear in search at all. And ten or twenty tags reads as spam to both people and Facebook's own filters.

**Recommendation: two or three, or none.** If you use any, make them specific rather than broad. `#Ecommerce` is worthless. `#3PL` or `#ShopifySellers` at least describes an actual audience.

## What actually moves reach on Facebook

Worth saying plainly, because it matters far more than tags: **native video, especially Reels under 60 seconds with a strong hook in the first three seconds, gets the biggest algorithmic push.** After that, **comments in the first 30 to 60 minutes** matter more than almost anything else.

That is why three of these four Facebook posts end on a real question. It is not filler, it is the mechanism.

It also suggests something worth considering. Post 1 on dimensional weight would work considerably better as a 40-second video of someone at a packing bench putting a small product into an oversized box, then into a right-sized one. Same argument, native format, far more reach.

## An honest note on Facebook as a channel for this

Organic reach for a B2B page on Facebook is poor and has been for a long time. Your buyers are Shopify, TikTok Shop and crowdfunding sellers, and on Facebook those people are mostly **in Groups, not following brand pages**.

These adaptations are worth posting. But the same content dropped into relevant seller Groups, where the rules allow it and where you are a participant rather than an advertiser, will almost certainly outperform your own page. Post 4 in particular, the "which parts will you not handle" one, is exactly the kind of thing that does well in a seller community, because it helps people evaluate everyone rather than selling them anything.
