## POST 1 — Dimensional weight
Source: `dim-weight-packaging-optimisation-2026`
Suggested image: the DIM weight infographic from that post

---

On 12 July, USPS changed how it charges for parcel size. Most sellers have not noticed, because it arrives as a slightly larger invoice rather than an announcement.

Two things changed. The divisor moved from 166 to 139, which reporting at the time put at roughly a 19 percent increase in billable weight on affected parcels. And fractional measurements now round up rather than to the nearest inch, so a carton measuring 12.1 inches is billed as 13. Across three dimensions that adds up before the divisor is even applied.

It only bites on parcels over one cubic foot. Which makes that line a genuinely useful thing to design packaging around.

Here is the part worth sitting with. Dimensional weight bills you for the space a parcel occupies, not what is in it. A 30 x 30 x 30 box holding a 400g product is not a 400g shipment as far as your carrier is concerned. You are paying to ship air.

The categories hit hardest are predictable. Apparel, bedding, soft furnishings, toys, pet products, anything sold as a multipack, and anything shipped in a box chosen for how it looks rather than how it fits.

The fix is not a cheaper carrier. It is a smaller box, and boxes are chosen at the packing bench.

Right-size the carton. Most brands run three or four box sizes because that is what they ordered originally, then keep using them as the range grows. Audit your actual SKU dimensions against your actual carton range and you will usually find several products shipping a size too big, on every order, forever.

Move soft goods to poly mailers. A mailer takes the shape of its contents, which removes most of the penalty outright.

Compress the void fill rather than just filling it. Air pillows are cheap but they set the minimum box size. The goal is a product that cannot move, not a box that is full.

You renegotiate carrier rates once a year. You choose a box size on every single order.

## POST 2 — Returns
Source: `ecommerce-returns-fulfilling-from-china-2026`
Suggested image: the returns loop diagram from that post

---

The most common reason brands rule out fulfilling from China is returns. It is also the easiest of their worries to solve.

The objection usually pictures the naive version: a customer in Ohio posts a $25 item back to Shenzhen individually. That costs more in international postage than the item is worth, takes weeks, and still arrives as something you have to discount. Quite right to rule it out. Nobody should run it that way.

Some numbers to size the problem honestly. An all-in parcel return in 2026 commonly costs $10 to $30 once you count reverse shipping, processing labour, restocking and markdown. Reverse logistics can eat 20 to 30 percent of the original product value. Apparel return rates routinely run 20 to 40 percent, and roughly a third of returned apparel fails inspection and has to be marked down heavily, donated or destroyed. A return is not a unit back on the shelf.

So invert it. Your customer never returns to China. They return to a local address in their own country, cheaply and quickly, exactly the experience they expect. The decisions then happen there, in bulk.

At that hub every item goes one of three ways, and the right call is arithmetic rather than habit. Resellable and you hold stock in that market: restock locally, no border, no return freight. Worth recovering but not resellable there: hold it and send a consolidated pallet back to Shenzhen periodically, because batched freight per unit is a fraction of individual postage. Neither: liquidate or dispose in-market, because paying to move a dead unit across the world is throwing good money after bad.

Two levers most sellers miss.

Set a value threshold below which refunds are returnless. When recovering and reselling an item costs more than the item is worth, letting the customer keep it is both cheaper and better for satisfaction.

And look at duty drawback. Now that de minimis has ended you are paying import duty on goods entering the US, and when some of those are later re-exported a portion may be recoverable. Estimates put it at 15 to 25 percent of landed cost on eligible returns. It is a specialised customs process worth raising with a drawback specialist, and the thing that makes or breaks a claim is clean documentation.

Build the loop in on day one rather than bolting it on when returns start piling up.

## POST 3 — Incoterms and EXW
Source: `incoterms-china-imports-exw-fob-cif-ddp`
Suggested image: the Incoterms comparison infographic

---

The cheapest quote your supplier gives you is usually the most expensive one.

EXW looks lower on the proforma because the origin costs have been stripped out of it. They have not gone anywhere. You will pay them later, itemised, and by then they are yours to chase:

Inland haulage from the factory to the port. Chinese export customs clearance and documentation. Terminal handling at origin. Origin agent fees.

Add those back and EXW frequently lands above the FOB price for the same goods, with more paperwork and more places to be delayed.

There is a second cost that does not show up on any invoice. Under EXW, if export clearance goes wrong, it is your problem, in a jurisdiction where you have no standing and probably no Mandarin. Under FOB the supplier owns that leg, and they are on the ground.

EXW does earn its place in one situation. If you already have a forwarder or partner in China handling the origin leg for you, then you are effectively recreating FOB with a provider you chose rather than one your supplier chose. That is a legitimate reason to use it, and a good one.

Wanting a smaller number on the proforma is not.

If you are comparing supplier quotes this week, the useful exercise is to add the origin costs back onto every EXW price before you compare anything. The ranking often changes.

## POST 4 — The question providers dread
Source: `how-to-choose-a-china-3pl`
Suggested image: the evaluation checklist infographic

---

The most useful question you can ask a fulfilment provider is the one they least want to answer.

Which parts will you not handle?

An honest answer from any China 3PL includes at least these. You are the importer of record. You arrange your own customs bond where one is required. Product testing and certification sit with you and your manufacturer, through accredited third-party labs. Tax registration is yours, whether that is UK or EU VAT, IOSS, or Japanese consumption tax.

A warehouse can prep your goods correctly, document them accurately and hand them to a carrier. It cannot become your legal importer or certify your product. Any provider implying otherwise is either confused or hoping you are, and you will find out which at the worst possible moment.

A second question worth stealing, because it is quietly brutal and it works: can you describe my marketplace's prep spec without looking it up?

Amazon, Walmart and TikTok Shop each have their own barcoding, labelling, poly-bagging and carton rules, and they differ in exactly the details that get shipments rejected. Someone who does this daily will talk fluently about FNSKU placement, suffocation warnings, master carton labels and box content accuracy. Someone who does not will talk about "full prep services".

And ask for the whole rate card in writing, not just the pick and pack rate. Receiving, storage after any free period, per-unit prep, returns handling, minimums. What you are really testing is whether a provider is comfortable being compared.

Ask these of everyone you are considering. Including us.
