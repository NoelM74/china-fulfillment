China to the Middle East (2026): VAT, the GCC Customs Union and the COD Reality

By Noel Murphy Published October 20, 2026

Quick answer: Six markets worth serving from one warehouse: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt, Qatar, Bahrain and Kuwait. The five Gulf states share a customs union with a common external tariff, commonly 5 percent of CIF. They do not share a tax system: VAT runs from 15 percent in Saudi Arabia to nothing at all in Qatar and Kuwait. Egypt sits outside the union entirely with 14 percent VAT and duty from zero to 60 percent. And if you sell cash on delivery, the number that decides whether you make money is not shipping cost. It is how often parcels come back.

The Middle East gets treated as one market by sellers and as six by customs authorities. That gap is where the margin goes.

Two things fall out of that table immediately.

Duty is the easy part in the Gulf. A customs union with a common external tariff means the same product generally attracts the same duty whether it lands in Doha or Dubai. That is genuinely simpler than the EU, where you still face national VAT complexity on top, or the US, where classification decides everything.

Tax is where they diverge, sharply. A product landing at the same CIF value carries 15 percent VAT in Riyadh, 5 percent in Dubai and nothing in Doha or Kuwait City. That is not a rounding difference. It is a pricing decision.

The mistake that catches sellers

The GCC is a customs union. It is not a single tax market, and the two get conflated constantly.

Selling cross-border from the UAE into Saudi Arabia does not count as a sale within one internal market. It creates Saudi tax obligations. Sellers who set up in the UAE because the VAT rate is low, then serve Saudi customers from there, tend to discover this after the fact rather than before.

If you are selling into more than one Gulf state, treat each as its own tax jurisdiction and take advice on registration thresholds in each. Tax registration is yours, not your fulfilment provider's, exactly as it is with UK and EU VAT or Japanese consumption tax.

Egypt is a different proposition

It is worth separating Egypt out rather than bundling it under "Middle East", because almost nothing about it matches the Gulf.

It is not in the GCC customs union, so the flat 5 percent does not apply. Duty averages around 10.5 percent but ranges from zero to 60 percent depending on the product, which puts classification back at the centre of your landed cost in a way it is not in the Gulf.

VAT is 14 percent, calculated on CIF value plus customs duty and other taxes, so it compounds on the duty rather than sitting beside it.

And there is no de minimis for commercial shipments. A low-value threshold exists for personal effects and gifts, commonly cited around EGP 2,000 or one kilogram, but it does not cover commercial ecommerce. Charges apply from the first dollar.

Egypt also runs a single-window customs system, and pre-shipment registration requirements have applied to certain shipments since 2021. If Egypt is more than an experiment for you, that is a conversation to have with a customs broker before volume rather than after.

The part that actually decides your margin

Everything above is arithmetic you can do once. This next part you live with every day.

Cash on delivery is normal across this region in a way it is not in Western markets. Published figures vary a lot depending on what is being measured, but the direction is consistent: COD remains materially more common in Egypt than in the Gulf, and more common across the region than in Europe or North America. Reported COD shares for Egypt run anywhere from roughly half to around two thirds of transactions depending on the source and the segment, with Gulf figures lower and falling.

Do not take any single number as gospel here. Some sources measure share of completed transactions, others measure stated customer preference, and they produce very different answers for the same country.

What matters commercially is the other side of COD.

RTO is the real cost

When a customer refuses a cash on delivery parcel at the door, the courier marks it a failed delivery and sends it back. You paid to ship it, you pay to bring it back, and you collect nothing. That is return to origin, and it is the line item that decides whether COD works for your product.

Reported RTO rates by market, with the same caution about sources disagreeing:

Market Reported RTO range
Egypt Roughly 15% to 35%
Saudi Arabia Roughly 15% to 25%
UAE Roughly 10% to 18%

Those ranges are wide and the sources do not reconcile. Treat them as directional: Egypt highest, UAE lowest, Saudi in between.

The commercial point survives the imprecision. A product with a 12 percent RTO rate and a product with a 30 percent RTO rate are different businesses, even at identical shipping cost. Cheaper delivery in Egypt, commonly quoted well below Gulf rates per shipment, is partly offset by parcels coming back more often.

This is why we track RTO by country and product category rather than quoting you a delivery rate and leaving you to find out. A market that looks cheap on the shipping line can be the worst one you sell in.

Reducing RTO

The levers are unglamorous and they work.

Confirm the order before dispatch. A message or call that gets a reply is a parcel far more likely to be accepted.

Offer card payment at the door as well as cash. Our courier partners accept cash, POS card, mobile wallet and card payments depending on market. More ways to pay is fewer refusals.

Watch which products refuse. RTO is not uniform across a catalogue. Impulse categories and higher price points refuse more. That pattern is visible in the data long before it is visible in your bank balance.

Be precise about delivery windows. Much COD refusal is not rejection of the product, it is nobody home.

Why fulfil from China rather than hold stock locally

The honest answer is that it depends on your volume.

Holding stock in a Dubai or Riyadh warehouse gives you next-day delivery and a local returns address. If you are shipping serious volume into one Gulf market, that is worth costing.

You pay to move a unit when someone buys it, not months in advance. Orders from a Shopify store, a marketplace listing and a COD campaign all draw from the same shelf, which is the same logic as multi-channel fulfilment anywhere.

It also means you can test a market properly. Adding Bahrain to a campaign is a routing decision, not a warehouse lease.

The bottom line

Six markets, one warehouse, and two numbers that decide everything.

Duty is straightforward across the Gulf thanks to the customs union. VAT is not, and it ranges from 15 percent to zero, so price each market separately. Egypt is a different regime entirely and deserves its own analysis rather than being lumped in.

And if you sell cash on delivery, watch RTO more closely than you watch shipping rates. It varies more, it moves more, and it is the number that quietly decides whether a market is worth being in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Middle East markets can you fulfil from China?

Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Egypt, Qatar, Bahrain and Kuwait. Both prepaid and cash on delivery are available in all six, shipped from our Shenzhen warehouse with local courier partners handling final delivery and, for COD orders, collection at the door.

Is the GCC a single market for customs and tax?

For customs, largely yes. The GCC operates a customs union with a common external tariff, commonly 5 percent of CIF value on most goods. For tax, no. VAT is set nationally and varies widely: 15 percent in Saudi Arabia, 10 percent in Bahrain, 5 percent in the UAE, and no domestic VAT in Qatar or Kuwait as at 2026. Selling cross-border from the UAE into Saudi Arabia creates Saudi tax obligations rather than counting as one internal market.

Does Egypt work the same way as the Gulf states?

No. Egypt is not a GCC member and sits outside the customs union. VAT is 14 percent, calculated on CIF value plus customs duty and other taxes. Duty rates vary far more widely than the GCC's flat 5 percent, with an average around 10.5 percent and a range from zero to 60 percent depending on the product. There is no de minimis for commercial shipments, so charges apply from the first dollar.

How does cash on delivery work when shipping from China?

Stock sits in our Shenzhen warehouse. When an order comes in we pick, pack and ship it, and a local courier partner in the destination country collects payment from the customer at the door in cash or by card. Collected funds are pooled and settled to your account on a weekly cycle, with statements showing every collection.

What is RTO and why does it matter more than shipping cost?

Return to origin is what happens when a customer refuses a cash on delivery parcel at the door. You have paid to ship it, you pay to bring it back, and you collect nothing. Published RTO rates vary substantially by market, with Egypt reported highest and the UAE lowest, so the same product can have very different unit economics in two countries with similar shipping costs.
