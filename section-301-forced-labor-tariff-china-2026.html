The 12.5% Tariff on China Is in Court. Here Is What You Pay Until It Isn't.

By Noel Murphy Published October 3, 2026

Quick answer: Since 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on 24 July 2026, goods of Chinese origin entering the US pay an additional 12.5% Section 301 forced labor duty. It replaced the expired 10% Section 122 surcharge, and it stacks on top of the normal duty and the existing China Section 301 tariffs of 7.5% to 100%. More than 2,000 tariff codes are on an exemption list, and Section 232 goods are excluded. Unlike the tariff it replaced, it has no built-in expiry. It is, however, being challenged: the Court of International Trade heard arguments on 30 September 2026 from businesses and 25 states seeking its removal and refunds. Keep your entry records.

On 24 July your duty on Chinese goods went up by two and a half points, and most sellers did not notice.

That is the small part of the story. The bigger part is that the tariff underneath changed from one with a legal expiry date to one without, and that the new one is now in front of a federal court with refunds at stake.

What happened on 24 July

The US Trade Representative took final action on 23 July 2026 after investigations into 60 economies that, in USTR's finding, had not established or effectively enforced bans on imports made with forced labor.

From 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on 24 July 2026, goods from those economies pay an additional duty:

Group Additional duty
Economies with a forced labor import prohibition in place 10%
Economies without one 12.5%

China is in the 12.5% group. So is Vietnam, which matters if you were counting on a move south to escape it.

The 60 economies between them account for roughly 99.4% of US imports. In effect, this is close to a universal tariff, applied through the forced labor statute.

Why this one is harder to wait out

The new duty replaced the 10% Section 122 surcharge, which expired the same day. That surcharge had itself been put in place after the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026.

Here is the difference that matters. Section 122 tariffs are limited by statute to 150 days unless Congress votes to extend them. Its expiry was built in from the day it started.

Section 301 actions carry no such automatic end date. The China Section 301 tariffs imposed in 2018 and 2019 are still in force years later.

So 24 July was not just a 2.5 point increase. It was the moment a temporary tariff became one with no expiry written into it. If your pricing assumed the surcharge would lapse and take your costs back down, that assumption no longer holds.

How your duty now stacks

The forced labor duty is additional. It does not replace anything you were already paying except the expired Section 122 surcharge. For a typical consumer product from China, three layers now apply:

  1. The normal duty for the product's HTS code, known as the MFN rate
  2. The existing China Section 301 tariff, from 7.5% to 100% depending on the product's list
  3. The new 12.5% forced labor duty

A worked example

Take a shipment with a customs value of $10,000, an MFN rate of 5%, on Section 301 List 4A at 7.5%. The figures are illustrative; your own depend on your codes.

Layer Rate Duty
MFN duty 5% $500
Section 301, List 4A 7.5% $750
Section 301, forced labor 12.5% $1,250
Total 25% $2,500

Under the 10% Section 122 surcharge, the same shipment paid $2,250. The change cost $250 per $10,000 of goods. Small on one entry. Over a year of containers, it is a line in your accounts.

That is also before customs processing fees, and it assumes a List 4A product. A product on a 25% list pays considerably more, which is why getting the classification right has become one of the few levers left.

What is exempt

Not everything pays. The final action includes an exemption list:

  • More than 2,000 tariff codes are on the list, several hundred of them fully exempt
  • Others are exempt only for specific uses, such as civil aircraft or pharmaceutical use
  • Goods covered by enumerated Section 232 programmes are excluded from the forced labor duty

The practical point: exemption follows the HTS code, not the product description. Two products that look similar can sit under different codes with different outcomes. If you think your goods might qualify, check the code, and if the classification is genuinely uncertain, a CBP binding ruling is the way to get certainty you can rely on.

The court case, and why your paperwork matters now

On 30 September 2026, a three-judge panel of the US Court of International Trade heard challenges to the forced labor tariffs. The plaintiffs are small businesses and a coalition of 25 states.

Their argument, broadly, is that USTR imposed a near-universal tariff without the country-specific findings and reasoned explanation that Section 301 requires, and used forced labor as a pretext. They are asking for the tariffs to be removed and for refunds. The Department of Justice argues the action is consistent with the text and purpose of the statute.

There is no ruling yet. Whichever way it goes, an appeal is likely, so do not reprice on the assumption of a quick win.

What you can do is make sure that if refunds ever become available, you are in a position to claim them:

  • Keep complete entry records for everything that has paid the forced labor duty since 24 July: entry summaries, commercial invoices, HTS codes, duty paid
  • Ask your customs broker now whether you need to take any step to keep a refund claim open, such as watching liquidation dates on your entries

That second point is a question for a licensed broker or trade attorney, not something to guess at. The cost of asking is one email. The cost of finding out later that a deadline passed is the refund.

Origin is not where it ships from

Every tariff increase on China brings the same question: can the goods go via somewhere else?

Country of origin is where goods are made, or substantially transformed. It is not where they are stored, packed or shipped from. Goods made in China and shipped from our warehouse in Shenzhen are Chinese origin. Goods made in China, sent to a third country, relabelled and shipped on are still Chinese origin, and declaring otherwise is illegal transshipment, which CBP actively pursues.

Genuine production in another country is different, and for some products it changes the older China Section 301 exposure. But it does not change the forced labor duty if that country is also in the 12.5% group, as Vietnam is. Run the numbers on real origin, not on paperwork.

The bottom line

Your duty on Chinese goods rose 2.5 points on 24 July and moved onto a legal footing with no expiry date. It now stacks on everything you were already paying.

It may not last. A federal court heard the case three days ago, and refunds are on the table. But "may not last" is not a pricing strategy. Price for the tariff you pay today, check whether your codes are exempt, and keep every entry record as if you will need it, because you might.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Section 301 forced labor tariff?

It is an additional US import duty imposed by the Office of the United States Trade Representative under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, on goods from 60 economies that USTR found had not established or enforced bans on imports made with forced labor. It applies to goods entered for consumption from 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time on 24 July 2026. The rate is 10% for economies with a forced labor import prohibition and 12.5% for those without one.

What rate applies to goods from China?

China is in the group paying 12.5%. That replaced the 10% Section 122 surcharge, which expired on 24 July 2026, so the headline rate on Chinese goods rose by 2.5 percentage points.

Does the 12.5% stack on top of the existing Section 301 China tariffs?

Yes. The forced labor duty applies in addition to the existing Section 301 duties on imports from China, which range from 7.5% to 100% depending on the product and its list. It also sits on top of the normal most favoured nation duty for the product's tariff code.

Are any goods exempt from the forced labor duty?

Yes. The final action includes an exemption list covering more than 2,000 tariff codes, of which several hundred are fully exempt and others are exempt only for specific uses such as civil aircraft or pharmaceuticals. Goods covered by enumerated Section 232 programmes are also excluded. Whether your product qualifies depends on its exact HTS classification, so check the code rather than the product description.

Is the forced labor tariff being challenged in court?

Yes. A three-judge panel of the US Court of International Trade heard arguments on 30 September 2026 from small businesses and a coalition of 25 states, who argue USTR exceeded its authority and seek removal of the tariffs and refunds. The Department of Justice is defending the action. There is no ruling yet, and any decision may be appealed.

Does moving production to Vietnam avoid the tariff?

Not this one. Vietnam is also in the 12.5% group. What changes is the older China-specific Section 301 tariff lists, which apply to goods of Chinese origin and not to genuine Vietnamese origin. Origin is decided by where goods are made or substantially transformed, not where they ship from, and routing Chinese goods through another country to change their declared origin is illegal transshipment.
